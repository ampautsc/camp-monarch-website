# Camp Monarch — Page Design Document

*This document is the constraint Sis checks before touching any page. Before editing, adding, or removing any section, read the purpose and bones for that page. If a proposed change doesn't serve the stated purpose, it doesn't belong on that page — it belongs on a different page, or not at all.*

*Last updated: 2026-05-16. Update when a page's purpose is intentionally changed — not when content is added.*

---

## Site-Level Transformation Arc

**Visitor arrives as:** Someone who vaguely cares about nature but has no specific awareness of what is happening in their own yard, no path to action, and no sense that their property could matter.

**Visitor leaves as:** Someone who understands the specific stakes, has made one concrete decision about their yard, and knows where to come back when they're ready for the next step.

**The arc across pages:**
1. **Crisis + stakes** (Why Monarchs, Home hero) — break normalization; make decline feel real and personal
2. **The lever** (Home → Plant Milkweed) — one action, credibly described; prove that a single yard can matter
3. **The experience** (The First Year) — what it actually feels like; normalize the journey; reduce hesitation
4. **The ecosystem** (Species Gallery, all neighbor pages) — your yard is already part of something; these animals need you
5. **Deeper commitment** (Habitat Transformation, Take Action, Practical Guides) — for people who said yes and want more
6. **Sustained engagement** (Seasonal Calendar, Spring Checklist, Log a Sighting) — reasons to return

## Core Monarch Pages

### Home (`/`)

**Purpose:** Make a first-time visitor feel the gap and see the lever — in 90 seconds.

**Visitor:** Anyone. This is the front door. Most will be curious but uncommitted.

**Bones:**
1. **Hero** — Crisis stated in two numbers. Not paragraphs. The gap between then and now, rendered as a fact. Visitor question answered: *Is this real?* → Yes.
2. **Lever** — One plant, one reason. Milkweed dependency explained. Visitor question answered: *Can one yard matter?* → Yes.
3. **How You Can Help** — Four action cards with photos. Each answers a different visitor mode: plant something / watch and report / talk to someone / add more plants. Visitor question answered: *What should I do first?* → Here are four paths; pick one.
4. **Meet Your Neighbors** — Species preview. Four cards with wonder hooks. Visitor question answered: *Is this just about monarchs?* → No. Your yard is already home to more than you know. Leads to species gallery.
5. **Right Now** — Seasonal panel. One or two things to do this specific month. Visitor question answered: *What do I do this week?*
6. **Social proof** — iNaturalist count. Others are already doing this. Visitor question answered: *Am I alone in this?* → No.

**Exit state:** Visitor has either clicked into Plant Milkweed, Meet Your Neighbors, or one of the How You Can Help paths. If they leave without clicking, they at least know the stakes and have a reason to return.

**Do not add:** Pages about organizational history, donation appeals, or abstract vision statements. This page is for people who just arrived. It is not a newsletter.

### Why Monarchs (`/why-monarchs`)

**Purpose:** Break normalization — make 80% decline feel like loss, not background noise.

**Visitor:** Someone who already knows monarchs are declining but hasn't felt it yet. Or someone who clicked "learn more" from the home hero. They need to feel before they act.

**Bones:**
1. **Hero** — Headline names decline AND broadens it: "They are not alone." Sets up the ecosystem argument before it's made.
2. **The numbers** — Population data, named sources, specific overwintering site counts. Stakes made precise. Not just "they're declining" but "here is the exact number."
3. **Why monarchs are the right signal** — They are a proxy for the whole pollinator ecosystem. Milkweed loss → monarch decline → proxy for habitat loss at scale. This is why monarchs matter beyond themselves.
4. **What you may not have known you forgot** — Normalization break. Generational memory of abundance. The reader probably remembers more monarchs as a child than they see now. Name that.
5. **Three things that make a difference** — Transition to action. Don't leave someone in grief. Give them a path out of knowing into doing.

**Exit state:** Visitor understands why monarchs are the right species to focus on, has felt the loss personally rather than just understood it abstractly, and knows there is something specific they can do.

**Do not add:** General information about monarch biology that belongs on Monarch Life. General gardening advice that belongs on Choose a Plant. This page is about stakes, not biology.

### Monarch Life (`/monarch-life`)

**Purpose:** Make the monarch's biology miraculous — create the attachment that sustains action.

**Visitor:** Someone who already cares and wants to understand more deeply. The curious newcomer who asks "but how does it work?"

**Bones:**
1. Hero — Wonder statement. Not "monarchs are beautiful" but something specific about the biology that surprises.
2. Life cycle — egg → caterpillar → chrysalis → adult. Each stage with a specific surprising detail (not just the standard facts).
3. The navigation mystery — Sun compass + magnetic compass in the antennae. The generation that migrates has never made the trip before. This is the fact that makes people stop.
4. Threats — Brief. OE parasite, milkweed loss, climate timing mismatch. Specific, not generic.
5. What you can do — Short. Links to Plant Milkweed, Take Action, Log a Sighting.

**Exit state:** Visitor feels genuine wonder about a monarch's actual life. They are now attached to this animal as an individual, not just a species statistic.

### The Migration (`/the-migration`)

**Purpose:** Show the scale of the journey — transform "butterfly migration" into something that feels impossible and therefore urgent.

**Visitor:** Someone already engaged who wants to understand the stakes of the migration route specifically.

**Bones:**
1. Scale and impossibility — 2,500 miles, 4 generations, navigates to a specific mountain range in Mexico.
2. The corridor — The migration relies on a continuous chain of nectar and milkweed. Break one link and the chain fails.
3. Where the corridor is failing — Specific regions, specific causes.
4. What a yard in the corridor does — Your yard is a link in a chain that crosses a continent.
5. Action — Log a sighting. Plant milkweed on the route. Links out.

**Exit state:** Visitor understands that every yard in the flyway is either a link or a gap. Their yard is already one of those things; the question is which.

### The First Year (`/the-first-year`)

**Purpose:** Reduce hesitation by naming the real experience — what it feels like, month by month, to actually do this.

**Visitor:** Someone who said yes in principle but hasn't started. They are held back by uncertainty about what will actually happen. This page is the bridge from intention to first action.

**Bones:**
1. **Hero** — Name the witnessing gap: *You have probably seen fewer monarchs than you used to.* This is the moment of recognition that makes what follows feel personal.
2. **The year, month by month** — Not a how-to guide. A lived account. What you notice in April. What surprises you in June. What you feel in September when the migration passes through. The specific details that make it real.
3. **What changes after the first year** — The shift from "am I doing this right?" to "the yard changed." The quiet evidence that it worked.
4. **What people say about their first season** — Testimonials. Not "I loved it" but specific moments: *I found a chrysalis on my fence post in August. I didn't know what it was at first.*
5. **What no one tells you** — Honest admissions. Milkweed looks weedy. Neighbors will ask questions. Some years are better than others. This is the page that earns trust.
6. **Ready to start?** + **Log what you see** — CTAs. Plant milkweed + log your first sighting.

**Exit state:** Visitor no longer imagines a polished, expert-managed garden. They imagine their actual yard, with specific plants, and can picture themselves doing this. The anxiety is replaced by a concrete first step.

**This is the model page for Camp Monarch content.** Every page should aspire to its specificity and honest tone. If a page is too abstract, ask: what would the first-year equivalent of this section look like?

### Plant Milkweed (`/plant-milkweed`)

**Purpose:** Get the visitor from "I want to plant milkweed" to "I know exactly which species and where to get it."

**Visitor:** Someone who is ready to act. They need a species recommendation for their region, not more persuasion.

**Bones:**
1. Brief stakes — One sentence. Why milkweed matters. This page doesn't need to re-argue the case.
2. Species finder — The functional core. Enter your state/region, get the right Asclepias species.
3. Why native species — Brief. Not all milkweed is equal. Tropical milkweed problem.
4. Where to get it — Native plant nurseries, Prairie Moon, local sources.
5. What to expect — Brief. First-year growth is modest. It comes back.

**Exit state:** Visitor knows which species to plant and where to buy it. They leave with a purchase decision, not just information.

### Raise a Monarch (`/raise-a-monarch`)

**Purpose:** Give the visitor the tools and confidence to raise monarchs at home — and make raising one feel like a participatory act of conservation, not a hobby.

**Visitor:** Someone who found a caterpillar, or who wants to try raising one. They want practical guidance.

**Bones:**
1. Why raising monarchs matters — OE parasite screening, boosting survival rates, direct connection to the animal.
2. What you need — Milkweed, enclosure, temperature.
3. The stages — Egg to caterpillar to chrysalis to eclosion. What to watch for, what's normal.
4. Tagging — Citizen science. If you're releasing, tag it.
5. Log the release — iNaturalist. Every tagged release is a data point.

**Exit state:** Visitor is confident they can do this. They know what success looks like and what to do if something goes wrong.

### Take Action (`/take-action`)

**Purpose:** Give the ready-to-act visitor a starting path when they don't know which entry point to choose.

**Visitor:** Someone who has already been persuaded but needs a concrete first step. They clicked "Take Action" from somewhere on the site.

**Bones:**
1. The situation in plain terms — Brief restatement of stakes. Not a re-argument. A reminder.
2. Five things you can do this week — Ordered by effort/impact. The first one (3×3 patch, one plant) is doable today. They escalate.
3. Track what you see — iNaturalist. Every sighting contributes to research.
4. Resources to get started — Links to Plant Milkweed, Choose a Plant, The First Year.
5. The most underrated step — Talk to a neighbor. Habitat is a corridor. One conversation creates two patches.

**Exit state:** Visitor leaves with one specific action decided. Ideally they have already clicked into Plant Milkweed or opened iNaturalist.

### Log a Sighting (`/log-a-sighting`)

**Purpose:** Reduce the friction between "I saw a monarch" and "I contributed to migration research."

**Visitor:** Someone who just saw a monarch. They may be on mobile in the yard right now.

**Bones:**
1. Why it matters — One sentence. Spring migration has no reporting network. Your sighting fills the gap.
2. How to log — iNaturalist steps, simplified. Photo optional but encouraged.
3. What happens to your data — It becomes open research data. Scientists use it.
4. Log yours now — Direct link to iNaturalist with pre-filled monarch search.

**Exit state:** Visitor has either logged a sighting or knows exactly how to. The barrier has been eliminated.

## Species Pages (template — applies to all 35)

All species pages follow a fixed argument structure. The template exists for a reason: it was designed to move a visitor from stranger to advocate for a specific animal they share their yard with. **Do not deviate from this structure without a documented reason in this file.**

### Species page template

**Purpose:** Transform a named animal from abstract concept to specific neighbor — and give the visitor one action they can take this week.

**Visitor:** Someone who followed a link from the homepage, the gallery, or another species page. They may not know this animal at all. They have thirty seconds of attention.

**Bones:**
1. **Hero image** — The animal, first. Before any text. The visitor must see it before they read about it. (web-design-001)
2. **Hero section** — The animal's name. One lead sentence that names a specific surprising biological fact — not "the monarch butterfly is beautiful" but "a firefly spends two years as a larva in your soil before it emerges for a few weeks as an adult." Visitor question: *Is this worth my attention?* → Here is one thing about this animal you did not know.
3. **What you are watching / The biology section** — The story that makes the animal real. Not facts as a list — facts as a narrative. The cecropia moth that cannot eat. The screech owl that hunts by sound in total darkness. The box turtle that may have lived in this yard for 40 years. Visitor question: *Why does this animal matter?*
4. **The threat / why it's declining** — Specific cause, specific number. Not "habitat loss" but "the 1906 introduction of Compsilura concinnata now parasitizes 80% of cecropia moths in Massachusetts." Make the threat feel concrete and reversible. Visitor question: *Is it too late?* → No. Here is exactly what is happening and why it is a decision, not an inevitability.
5. **Actions** — Three or four things, each with a specific detail that makes it actionable this week. Not "plant native plants" but "plant wild bergamot in a 4×4 patch in sun — it blooms June through August and a single plant will be covered with bumble bees within two weeks of first flower." Visitor question: *What do I do?* → This specific thing.
6. **Biology image + action image** — The 3-image minimum exists for a reason. Biology detail shows what the text describes. Action image shows what the action looks like.
7. **Species connections** — Links to 3 related pages. The visitor is shown that this animal shares habitat needs with animals they may already know. The ecosystem becomes visible.

**Exit state:** Visitor knows one specific surprising thing about this animal, understands why it is declining in terms of decisions not inevitabilities, and has identified at least one action they could take this week.

## Practical Guides

---

### Habitat Transformation (`/habitat-transformation`)

**Purpose:** Show that transforming a lawn into habitat is a process, not a project — anyone can start with four square feet this weekend.

**Visitor:** Someone who is convinced but intimidated. They imagine a complete landscape overhaul. This page shows them the first step is smaller than they think.

**Bones:**
1. The premise — A lawn is not the default. It is a choice that can be unmade in any corner, at any scale.
2. The five steps — Choose your patch → Smother the grass → Plant one native → Wait → Expand. Each step is specific and doable.
3. Species connections — Which animals benefit from each type of patch. Connects the habitat action to the neighbors it serves.
4. Starting small is not a compromise — Address the hesitation: "I don't have time to do the whole yard." Starting with four feet is not failure. It is the right beginning.

**Exit state:** Visitor can picture a specific corner of their yard and knows the first weekend's task. They are no longer imagining an overwhelming project.

---

### Choose a Plant (`/choose-a-plant`)

**Purpose:** Match visitor to the right native plant for their specific conditions — eliminate the "I don't know which plant to get" barrier.

**Visitor:** Someone who is ready to plant but paralyzed by choice. They need a recommendation, not a catalog.

**Bones:**
1. Tool — Conditions (sun/shade, wet/dry, region) → plant recommendations. Functional core.
2. Why native species specifically — One paragraph. Not ornamental native cultivars. The genetics matter.
3. Where to find it — Local native plant nurseries, regional mail-order sources.
4. What to expect in year one — Brief. It will look small. It is not dead.

**Exit state:** Visitor leaves with a specific plant name matched to their yard.

---

### Pesticide Guide (`/pesticide-guide`)

**Purpose:** Show the homeowner that the pest control choices made inside the property line reach far beyond the property line — and give them specific alternatives.

**Visitor:** Someone who uses pesticides without thinking about the downstream effects, or someone who wants to stop but doesn't know how.

**Bones:**
1. Three categories of harm — Neonicotinoids in nursery plants, glyphosate on lawn edges, broadcast mosquito sprays. Each named specifically.
2. Who it kills (for each category) — Not "insects" but specific species: monarch caterpillars, native bees, firefly larvae. Names the victim.
3. Alternatives (for each category) — Specific, practical. Not "choose organic" but "ask the nursery to show you plants grown without systemic insecticides."
4. The hardest case: mosquito sprays — Many people feel they have no choice. Name this honestly and give them the least-harm alternatives.

**Exit state:** Visitor can identify at least one pesticide use they are willing to change. They have a specific alternative ready.

---

### Water for Wildlife (`/water-for-wildlife`)

**Purpose:** Show that adding water to a yard is the highest-leverage action most homeowners haven't taken — and make the first step (a birdbath) as small as possible.

**Visitor:** Someone looking for the next step after planting milkweed or native flowers.

**Bones:**
1. Why water — Specific species that are blocked by lack of water (screech owl, bluebird, butterflies, bees). Name them.
2. Birdbath — The entry point. Specific height, specific depth, specific location (near cover, in shade). Not just "put out a birdbath."
3. Small pond — For the visitor ready for more. Specific dimensions, no liner required, what to plant around it.
4. Rain garden — For the visitor dealing with runoff. Connects water management to habitat.
5. What to expect — Species that will arrive, timeline.

**Exit state:** Visitor is adding at minimum a birdbath this week.

---

### Leave the Leaves (`/leave-the-leaves`)

**Purpose:** Reframe autumn raking as habitat destruction — and give the visitor permission to do less.

**Visitor:** The homeowner who rakes every fall without questioning it. This page is permission + explanation.

**Bones:**
1. What lives in leaf litter — Firefly larvae, native bee cocoons, overwintering moths, bird foraging. Be specific. Name the species.
2. What raking removes — Not leaves. Animals in their overwintering stage. Name the firefly larvae specifically. This is the emotional center.
3. The compromise — You don't have to stop raking everywhere. Leave litter in beds and under trees. Rake paths and lawn if you need to.
4. What you will see — What arrives in spring when you leave the leaves. The reward.

**Exit state:** Visitor decides to leave at least one section of leaves this fall. The permission has been given.

---

### HOA Guide (`/hoa-guide`)

**Purpose:** Give the homeowner the specific language and process to get native planting approved by an HOA or skeptical neighbor.

**Visitor:** Someone who wants to act but faces social or regulatory friction. They need a script, not inspiration.

**Bones:**
1. How to approach the conversation — Lead with shared values (property values, water runoff, beauty), not ecology lecture.
2. The checklist process — How to frame a proposal in terms HOA boards respond to.
3. Talking points script — Specific language for specific objections ("it looks messy," "it will spread to my yard," "it attracts pests").
4. What to show — Before/after examples, certified plant lists, neighbor success stories.

**Exit state:** Visitor has a specific script and a process. The HOA conversation feels manageable.

### Invasive Plants (`/invasive-plants`)

**Purpose:** Help the homeowner identify and remove the plants that are actively blocking native habitat — and prioritize which to address first.

**Visitor:** Someone who suspects they have invasive plants, or who just learned that certain common plants are a problem.

**Bones:**
1. Why it matters — An invasive plant doesn't just occupy space; it suppresses the natives that wildlife depends on. Specific example (e.g., burning bush, Japanese barberry, privet).
2. The most common offenders — Regional. Named specifically with photos.
3. How to remove — Each species is different. Brief specific guidance, or links to detailed removal resources.
4. What to plant instead — Native alternatives for each invasive. One-for-one replacement frames this as addition, not subtraction.

**Exit state:** Visitor has identified at least one invasive plant in their yard and knows the native replacement.

### Spring Checklist (`/spring-checklist`)

**Purpose:** Give the habitat-builder a concrete seasonal task list to start the year right.

**Visitor:** A returning visitor who planted milkweed last year and wants to know what to do this spring.

**Bones:**
1. Priority tasks — Ordered by timing and impact. First: check milkweed emergence. Second: clean the birdbath. Third: order native plants before they sell out.

### Habitat Score (`/habitat-score`)

**Purpose:** Give the homeowner a way to measure their progress — transform "I'm trying to do better" into a specific, improvable number.

**Visitor:** Someone already engaged who wants feedback on where they are and what to do next.

**Bones:**
1. The score — Input current yard conditions → output score with category breakdown.
2. What the score means — Not a grade. A starting point. Most yards score 2–3 out of 10 today; a yard that took one year of serious action often reaches 6–7.
3. The next step — Based on score, recommend the highest-leverage next action.

**Exit state:** Visitor knows their current score and has identified one specific improvement that would raise it.

## Rules for Adding New Pages

Before creating any new page:

1. **Write its entry in this document first.** Name the purpose (≤10 words), the visitor, and the bones. If you cannot write the bones in advance, the page is not ready to be built.
2. **Check for overlap.** Does this page's purpose duplicate an existing page? If yes, add the content to the existing page instead.
3. **Check the site arc.** Where does this page fit in the six-stage arc above? If it doesn't fit anywhere in the arc, reconsider whether it belongs on this site.
4. **Run the argument test.** Can you state, for each section: "This section exists because the visitor just learned X and needs to know Y before they can act"? If a section doesn't answer that, it doesn't belong on the page.

---

## Rules for Editing Existing Pages

Before editing any page section:

1. **Read the bones for that page.** Does the proposed edit serve the stated purpose?
2. **Test each section transition.** What question did the previous section raise? Does this section answer it?
3. **Apply the copy-review skill** before publishing any change to body copy. (See `copy-review` skill.)
4. **Do not add sections that repeat what the hero already established.** The crisis is stated once. The lever is stated once. Each section advances — it does not re-argue.
